# Exercise metadata: sources found so far

Status: research only. Nothing here is stored in the code yet; how to store it (per-entry citations, a sources table, a review state) is still to be decided. Every source below was opened and read by the author of this note unless marked **not verified**. All studies are surface-EMG or small trials: EMG amplitude is not hypertrophy (see Plotkin 2023), so none of this rates how good an exercise is.

## How to read the "Effect on current metadata" column

- **supports**: the source agrees with the current classification.
- **changes**: the current classification should be corrected.
- **no source**: nothing found; the entry stays `uncertain` or `convention`.

## Shoulders

| Source | What it found | Effect on current metadata |
|---|---|---|
| Campos et al. 2020, *J Hum Kinet* 75:5–14 (13 trained men, 12 reps at 60% 1RM, sEMG) | Anterior deltoid highest in shoulder press (33%); bench press, lateral raise and fly ~19–21%. Medial deltoid highest in lateral raise (30%) and shoulder press (28%), very low in bench press (5%) and fly (3%). **Posterior deltoid highest in the lateral raise (24%)**, shoulder press 11%, bench and fly ~3%. | supports lateral raise → lateral and overhead press → anterior + lateral. **changes**: the lateral raise also loads the posterior deltoid, so its regions should be `lateral` plus `posterior`. Bench press → anterior deltoid at ~21% is a modest involvement; flat presses barely load medial/posterior. |
| Schoenfeld et al. 2013, *J Strength Cond Res* 27(10):2644–9 (19 trained men, reverse-fly machine) | Neutral grip gave higher posterior deltoid and infraspinatus activity than pronated grip. | supports reverse pec deck → posterior; adds grip as a variable (and infraspinatus as a second target). |
| Rodríguez-Ridao et al. 2020, *Int J Environ Res Public Health* 17(19):7339 (30 trained adults, 60% 1RM) | Anterior deltoid activity rises with bench incline and is highest at 60°. | supports incline press → anterior deltoid, increasing with angle. |
| Rabello et al. 2024 (systematic review, ScienceDirect) | Reported by a secondary web page: pull-ups and inverted rows give the greatest posterior deltoid activation among common exercises. | **not verified** (primary text not opened). Do not use yet. |

## Chest

| Source | What it found | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Rodríguez-Ridao et al. 2020 (above) | Clavicular head: highest at 30° (~30% MVIC), no significant difference between 0° and 45°. Sternal head: highest flat, falls as the incline rises. Only 0°–60° tested (no decline). | supports incline → clavicular, but only for a moderate angle (~30°); at 60° the anterior deltoid takes over. The catalogue does not record the angle. |
| Schanke 2012, UW-La Crosse MS thesis (not peer-reviewed) | Whole pec major, no regions. Bench press, pec deck and bent-forward cable crossover did not differ; seated chest press and incline dumbbell flye were lower. Dips and push-ups were not tested. | no source for dips, push-ups, flye and cable-crossover regions. Those stay `convention`/`uncertain`. |

## Back and lats

| Source | What it found | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Lehman et al. 2004, *Dynamic Medicine* 3:4 (12 men, self-selected loads, often <30–40% MVC) | Lats were *higher* in a seated row than in wide or reverse-grip pulldowns; grip changes made small, mostly non-significant differences; trapezius/rhomboids were highest in the seated row. The authors call "wide grip targets the back, supinated targets the biceps" unsupported. | supports rows → upper back (retractors). **changes**: the note on Wide-Grip Cable Row ("moves work from the lats to the upper back") has no support here and should be removed or marked unsourced. |
| Edelburg 2017, UW-La Crosse MS thesis (19 men, not peer-reviewed) | Lats highest in pull-ups and chin-ups. Middle trapezius highest in I-Y-T raises, bent-over, seated and inverted rows. Lower trapezius highest in I-Y-T raises only. Rhomboids and rear delts not measured. | supports vertical pull → lats and rows → middle trapezius. Low quality: thesis. |
| Ekstrom et al. 2003, *JOSPT* 33(5):247–58 (abstract only read) | Upper trapezius highest in the unilateral shrug. Middle trapezius highest in horizontal extension with external rotation (and prone overhead raise). Lower trapezius highest in the prone overhead raise. Serratus anterior highest in abduction above 120° in the scapular plane. | supports shrug → upper trapezius with `anatomy`/`convention`. Supports Y-raise → lower trapezius, **but for the prone version**; the standing Cable Y-Raise is still not covered, so it stays `uncertain`. |
| Face pull | **No primary EMG study of the face pull found.** A secondary page cites only studies of other exercises (Yoo 2017 pulling at 120° flexion for rhomboids; Tsuruike 2023 retraction exercises for lower trapezius and infraspinatus). | no source. All three face-pull entries stay `uncertain`; the claim of posterior deltoid + external rotators + retractors is a plausible extrapolation, not a finding. |

## Glutes and legs

| Source | What it found | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Contreras et al. 2015, *J Appl Biomech* 31(6) (13 trained women, 10RM) | Barbell hip thrust gave higher upper and lower gluteus maximus EMG than back squat (mean 69.5% vs 29.4% upper; 86.8% vs 45.4% lower). Vastus lateralis did not differ. No gluteus medius data. | supports hip thrust ≠ squat as different jobs for glute max. |
| Plotkin et al. 2023, *Front Physiol* 14, doi 10.3389/fphys.2023.1279170 (34 untrained adults, 9 weeks, MRI) | Hip thrust and back squat grew gluteus maximus about equally (upper, middle and lower). Gluteus medius/minimus grew little in both. Squat grew quadriceps and adductors more. Hip-thrust EMG was higher but did not predict growth. | **changes** the framing: "different job" is about movement, not about a better glute-max stimulus. The planner should not claim hip thrust is needed for glute growth. Supports not treating EMG as a rating. |
| Mausehund et al. 2018, *J Strength Cond Res* (11 trained people, 6–8RM) | Glute max and vastus lateralis: no difference between split squat, rear-foot-elevated split squat and single-leg squat. Gluteus medius highest in the single-leg squat (82% MVIC vs 55% RFESS, 46% split squat). Biceps femoris higher in RFESS. | supports lunge/split squat variants as the same job for glute max. **changes**: single-leg squat variants have a stronger gluteus medius component than split squats; the catalogue lists `medius` only for the hip abduction machine. |
| Boren et al. 2011, *Int J Sports Phys Ther* 6(3):206–23 | Rehabilitation exercises: side plank abduction, single-limb squat, clamshells and front plank with hip extension gave the highest gluteus medius (>70% MVIC). Machine hip abduction not tested. | supports medius work for abduction-type and single-leg exercises. No data on the machine itself. |
| Romanian vs conventional deadlift, *J Exerc Sci Fit* 2018 (authors not read) | Glute max activation was higher in the conventional deadlift (51.5%) than in the Romanian (46.9%); biceps femoris did not differ. | consistent with RDL being a hip-extension lift where glute max is not the dominant muscle. Keeps the existing note. |
| Hack squat, pendulum squat, leg press, front and low-bar squat | **No EMG source found** for glute involvement. | stay `convention`. |

## What this changes if applied

1. Lateral raise should name `posterior` as well as `lateral` for shoulders (Campos 2020). This alters same-job logic: a lateral raise and a reverse fly would overlap on the posterior deltoid.
2. Remove or mark unsourced the Wide-Grip Cable Row note.
3. Incline press → clavicular needs the angle to be meaningful (~30°); the catalogue has none.
4. Glute medius: add single-leg squat variants (Mausehund 2018, Boren 2011), not only the abduction machine.
5. "Hip thrust is a different job" is supported for activation (Contreras 2015) but not for hypertrophy (Plotkin 2023). The wording of the explanation must not promise more growth.
6. Face pull, standing Y-raise, cable crossover angle, dips, flyes, guillotine press: nothing found; keep `uncertain`.

## Not found, searched

Face pull (primary EMG), cable crossover angle, dips and push-ups by pec region, standing Y-raise, hack squat/pendulum/leg press glute involvement, lat regions (upper vs lower), serratus.

## Chest: incline press does not raise the upper chest

| Source | What it found | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| López-Vivancos et al. 2023, *Applied Sciences* 13:5203 (systematic review + meta-analysis) | Incline vs flat bench: **no significant difference in clavicular activity**; sternal activity significantly **lower** with incline. Decline: pooled results not significant (text and table disagree). Push-ups and other variants: bench produced more sternal activation. Dumbbell flye: clavicular similar to bench; sternal lower. Machine and standing cable presses: lower than bench. **Dips not analysed.** | **changes**: "incline → clavicular" is not supported as a higher upper-chest stimulus. What the data supports is "incline takes load off the sternal part". Downgrade the three incline entries from `convention` to `uncertain` and say so in the note, or describe them as "less sternal". Flyes and dips: still no source for region. |

## Shoulders: lateral raise variants and rear delts

| Source | What it found | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Coratella et al. 2020, *IJERPH* 17(17):6015 (competitive bodybuilders) | Medial deltoid highest in the neutral-rotation lateral raise. Anterior highest in the frontal raise. **Posterior deltoid and upper trapezius highest in the internally rotated lateral raise** (thumbs down / "pour the jug"). The study measured the medial deltoid; this project's "lateral" is the same head. | supports lateral raise → lateral (neutral rotation). **changes**: internal rotation shifts work to the posterior deltoid and upper trapezius, so the variants are not one job. The catalogue does not say which rotation each lateral raise uses. |
| Sweeney 2014, UW-La Crosse MS thesis (16 male lifters, not peer-reviewed) | Middle deltoid highest in the bent-arm lateral raise and the 45° incline row. Posterior deltoid highest in the seated rear lateral raise and the 45° incline row. Cable lateral raise, face pull and reverse fly were **not tested**. | supports rear-delt raise → posterior; shows a row can load the posterior deltoid as much as a raise. Does not cover cable lateral raise or the machine variants in the catalogue. |
| Frontiers in Physiology 2025, "Dumbbell versus cable lateral raises for lateral deltoid hypertrophy" (doi 10.3389/fphys.2025.1611468) | **Not read** (the full text would not load). Exists and is the direct evidence for cable lateral raises. | Still to read before touching the lateral-raise entries. |
| Tsuruike et al. 2023, *Int J Sports Phys Ther* (side-lying abduction) | With scapular retraction, lower trapezius reached 55% MVIC (vs 21% with protraction) and infraspinatus 41%; middle deltoid fell. Middle trapezius and rhomboids not measured. | Not a face-pull study. It shows that a retraction cue moves a shoulder exercise away from the middle deltoid and toward the lower trapezius/infraspinatus. |

## Traps: Y-raise

| Source | What it found | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hwang & Jeon 2024, *Isokinetics and Exercise Science* 32(3):281–9 (21 healthy men, band Y-raise) | Adding an isometric adduction cue **raised** lower trapezius and **lowered** upper trapezius and serratus anterior; the plain Y-raise therefore leaned more on the upper trapezius. Absolute values not in the abstract. | The current note ("lower traps may help") is not wrong but is incomplete: the lower-trap share depends on the cue, not the exercise. Keeps `uncertain`. Prone versions (Ekstrom 2003, Edelburg 2017) are better supported than the standing cable Y-raise. |

## Glutes and legs

| Source | What it found | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Contreras 2015, AUT PhD thesis | Parallel, full and front squats did not differ in EMG; barbell, American and band hip thrusts did not differ; barbell hip thrust gave much more hip-extensor and glute-max EMG than the parallel squat. A one-person twin study showed both lifts thickening the glutes. | supports putting squat variants in one job and hip-thrust variants in another. Squat depth did not change EMG in this thesis, which is at odds with the note "glute involvement grows with depth" on the back squat (that note currently has no source). |
| Leg press, hack squat, pendulum squat | **Still no primary source.** Searches returned equipment-blog pages only. One PMC page (PMC10349977) was blocked. | stays `convention`; consider recording "unsourced" rather than a region. |

## Face pull

Still no primary EMG study. Closest evidence is indirect: Schoenfeld 2013 (horizontal abduction, neutral grip raises posterior deltoid and infraspinatus), Tsuruike 2023 (retraction cue raises lower trapezius and infraspinatus), Ekstrom 2003 (horizontal extension with external rotation tops the middle trapezius). Together they make "posterior deltoid + external rotators + retractors" a reasonable inference, not a finding. Keep `uncertain`; say "inferred from three related studies".

## What the second round would change in the metadata

1. Incline press entries: lose the clavicular claim or say "less sternal, upper-chest effect not shown".
2. Lateral raise: add `posterior` (Campos 2020), and split by rotation (Coratella 2020); the catalogue needs a field for it.
3. Pullovers: stop labelling them lat isolation until a primary source is checked.
4. Back squat note on depth: remove or find a source.
5. Y-raise: record that the lower-trap share depends on a cue.
6. Face pull: keep, labelled "inferred".

Dips and push-up region data; cable-crossover angle; hack squat, pendulum squat and leg press glute data; the Frontiers 2025 cable vs dumbbell lateral raise result; a primary face-pull EMG study.
